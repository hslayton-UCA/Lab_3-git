Once upon a time, I went to the grocery store to buy some bread and milk. Everything was quiet until the store lights started flickering.
 Suddenly, a horde of zombies crashed through the front glass doors of the grocery store! The rain had turned the pine needles into grease, and the dirt underneath into quicksand. Candidate 104 felt the sandbags inside his ruck shifting left—a rookie mistake, packing top-heavy. He didn’t adjust them. Stopping meant falling behind the pace man, and falling behind meant getting dropped.

It was Day 11 of Special Forces Assessment and Selection (SFAS). No names, no ranks, no feedback. Just a roster number stamped on a white reflective band around his helmet and a cadre member silently writing in a waterproof notebook every time someone faltered.

His shoulders had stopped burning hours ago, replaced by a deep, hollow ache that vibrated in his collarbones. Every step of the land-navigation course was an exercise in precise misery. Navigate by terrain association, hold a dead reckon through a thicket of greenbrier that ripped at his uniform, check the compass. Repeat.

At the third point, a cadre member sat on the hood of a dusty Humvee, chewing on a toothpick.

"Roster 104," the cadre said, not looking up. "Turn in your card."

104 handed over the punch card, his hands shaking slightly from the cold sweat. The cadre marked it, handed it back, and pointed into the pitch-black tree line. "Next point is four kilometers east-northeast. Move."

No "good job." No indication if he was on time or twenty minutes late.

104 adjusted the heavy nylon straps cutting into his shoulders, took a breath of damp pine air, and stepped back into the dark. The instructors weren’t looking for supermen who never felt pain. They were looking for the guy who, when left entirely alone in the dark with no applause and no guarantee of success, chose to keep walking anyway.
